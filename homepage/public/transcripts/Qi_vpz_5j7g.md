Welcome  everyone.  Today  we  are  looking at  the  foundational  elements  of  the  ONNX ecosystem.  Specifically  focusing  on architecture,  governance  and  execution. As  shown  in  the  central  visualization, ONX  serves  as  the  vital  link  between various  development  frameworks  and deployment  environments.  Starting  with architecture,  ONX  provides  a standardized  model  format  that  allows for  seamless  interoperability. This  ensures  that  models  built  in different  frameworks  can  be  easily shared  and  utilized  without compatibility  hurdles.  Next,  we  have governance.  This  pillar  emphasizes  the open-source  communitydriven  nature  of the  project  by  maintaining  a  transparent and  collaborative  steering  process.  The ecosystem  remains  robust,  vendorneutral, and  aligned  with  industry  needs. Finally,  we  look  at  execution.  This  is the  stage  where  models  are  deployed  onto diverse  hardware  targets  through optimized  runtimes.  ONNX  enables  high performance  inference  across  everything from  cloud  servers  to  edge  devices  and specialized  silicon.  Together,  these three  components  form  a  comprehensive ecosystem  that  accelerates  AI  innovation and  simplifies  the  path  to  production. The  landscape  of  machine  learning  is currently  defined  by  significant fragmentation  between  various  training frameworks  and  deployment  runtimes.  This challenge  often  creates  barriers  when moving  a  model  from  the  research  phase into  a  production  environment.  To address  this,  the  open  neural  network exchange  or  ONNX  serves  as  the  universal representation  for  machine  learning.  Its mission  is  to  provide  a  standardized in-memory  computation  graph  that  ensures seamless  interoperability  and portability  of  AI  workloads  across diverse  environments.  By  establishing  a shared  intermediate  representation,  ONX effectively  decouples  model  creation from  hardware  execution.  This  solution allows  developers  to  train  models  in their  preferred  framework  and  deploy them  across  a  wide  range  of  hardware, including  CPUs,  GPUs,  and  edge  devices without  the  need  for  extensive  rewriting or  manual  optimization.  This  diagram illustrates  our  community  structure  and open  governance  model  organized  as  a series  of  concentric  tiers  that represent  increasing  levels  of responsibility  and  engagement.  At  the base,  we  have  the  members  who  are general  community  participants  committed to  adhering  to  our  code  of  conduct. Moving  a  step  inward  are  the contributors.  These  are  active participants  sponsored  by  two  approvers and  they  hold  the  community's  primary voting  rights.  Further  in  are  the approvers.  Individuals  in  this  role  must have  been  active  for  at least  3  months and  be  sponsored  by  a  special  interest group  or  SIG  chair.  Their  critical function  is  to  review  incoming contributions  for  quality  and  technical alignment.  At  the  center  is  the  steering committee  comprised  of  five  members elected  via  the  Condraay  voting  method. This  group  is  responsible  for  defining the  project's  overall  vision,  strategic goals,  and  governance  policies.  Finally, a  key  principle  of  our  governance  is  the distinction  regarding  influence.  Member companies  do  not  hold  direct  voting rights.  Instead,  influence  is  exercised through  individual  contributors.  This structure  is  intentionally  designed  to ensure  a  technical  meritocracy  where project  direction  is  determined  by  the expertise  and  activity  of  individuals rather  than  corporate  affiliation.  To understand  how  our  collaborative ecosystem  functions,  we  must  distinguish between  special  interest  groups  known  as SIGs  and  working  groups.  SIGs  are defined  by  their  persistent  domain ownership.  They  are  ongoing  entities that  provide  long-term  stewardship  over specific  architecture  parts,  taking direct  responsibility  for  code contributions  and  maintenance.  Working groups,  on  the  other  hand,  are established  for  temporary  crossboundary issue  resolution.  They  are  project focused  and  are  disbanded  once  their objectives  are  met.  Crucially,  working groups  do  not  own  long-term  artifacts. rather  they  develop  specifications, recommendations  and  implementations which  are  then  submitted  to  the appropriate  SIG  for  final  approval  and long-term  management.  The  core intermediate  representation  or  IR  is structured  around  three  fundamental components.  The  graph,  the  node,  and  the value.  The  graph  acts  as  the  primary container  encapsulating  the  entire computation  structure.  It  tracks  all inputs  and  outputs  while  ensuring  a strict  topological  ordering  of operations.  Within  this  structure,  we have  nodes  which  represent  individual operations  such  as  an  AD  or  a  matmo. These  nodes  are  versatile  and  can  store attributes  like  floating  point  values  or even  nested  graphs  for  more  complex logic.  Finally,  we  have  the  value.  This represents  the  data  flowing  between nodes.  Each  value  carries  crucial metadata  including  dimensions  and element  types.  providing  the  necessary context  for  how  data  moves  through  the graph.  This  slide  outlines  the methodologies  for  model  validation  and protobob  serialization.  Looking  at  the model  validation  workflow  on  the  left, we  utilize  a  funnel-like  process  where the  model  passes  through  a  checker context  and  a  lexical  scope  context  to produce  a  validated  graph.  This  approach allows  for  granular  checking  of components  down  to  the  tensor  proto  and node  proto  levels.  By  leveraging  the onyx  checker,  we  ensure  that  every  graph adheres  to  the  single  static  assignment or  SSA  form.  On  the  right,  we  see  the protobob  serialization  cycle  which manages  the  conversion  between  the  C++ internal  representation  and  the  onyx protobob  format.  This  is  handled  via export  model  proto  and  import  model proto.  A  vital  component  of  this  process is  external  data  security.  Our validation  includes  robust  path resolution  to  prevent  SIM  link  attacks, which  is  particularly  important  when  the system  is  loading  large  external  tensor files.  These  combined  steps  ensure  both the  integrity  and  the  security  of  the model  throughout  its  life  cycle.  The anatomy  of  an  operator  schema, specifically  an  op  schema  within  the AI.x  domain,  is  composed  of  five critical  structural  elements.  First, inputs  and  outputs  serve  as  the precisely  defined  data  entry  and  exit points  for  the  operation.  Next, attributes  provide  the  static configuration  parameters  that  determine specific  operator  behavior  to  ensure system  stability  and  prevent  execution faults.  Type  constraints  establish strict  rules  governing  the  allowed  data types  for  each  node.  The  documentation component  consists  of  embedded  C  style string  constants  that  are  automatically generated  into  Markdown  for  accessible technical  reference.  Finally,  the function  body  allows  for  the  creation  of complex  operators  by  composing  them  from simpler  existing  primitive  operators. Together,  these  components  ensure  that every  operator  is  robust,  well doumented,  and  functionally  consistent within  the  ecosystem.  This  slide  focuses on  the  vital  process  of  pre-runtime structural  integrity  achieved  through type  and  shape  inference.  By  analyzing input  shapes,  often  containing  symbolic dimensions  like  batch  size,  width,  and height,  the  system  utilizes  a  dedicated type  and  shape  inference  function  to propagate  structural  data  through  the model  graph.  This  phase  is  essential  for validating  the  model's  architecture before  execution  which  in  turn  enables precise  back-end  memory  allocation.  The inference  process  accounts  for sophisticated  operations  including tensor  broadcasting  and  complex  control flow  to  handle  variability.  The  symbol table  implementation  generates  unique symbolic  names  to  manage  unknown dimensions  consistently  across  the graph.  Furthermore,  our  approach  to dynamic  flow  ensures  that  complex subgraphs  within  operators  like  if, loop,  and  scan  are  correctly interpreted.  The  ultimate  goal  is  to produce  a  resolved  shape,  ensuring  that the  entire  computational  pipeline  is structurally  sound  and  optimized  for  the hardware  backend.  The  model  manipulation toolkit  offers  three  essential  features for  streamlining  and  customizing  neural networks.  Starting  with  function inlining,  this  technique  expands  defined function  calls  into  their  individual constituent  nodes.  It  handles  the complexities  of  variable  renaming  to maintain  uniqueness,  effectively flattening  the  model's  architecture  for improved  processing  and  clarity.  Next, size  reduction  is  a  powerful  tool  for generating  lightweight  versions  of models  intended  for  testing.  By replacing  massive  data  initializers  with more  efficient  dynamic  range  or  constant of  shape  sequences,  we  can  dramatically decrease  file  sizes  while  maintaining the  model's  functional  topology.  Lastly, dimension  updating  provides  the flexibility  needed  to  adapt  models  to varying  batch  sizes  or  dynamic resolutions.  This  ensures  that  a  model can  be  repurposed  for  different  hardware or  input  requirements  without  triggering validation  errors,  making  it  a  critical step  for  versatile  deployment.  The  upset version  conversion  framework  addresses  a fundamental  challenge  in  model evolution.  The  fact  that  operator signatures  change  over  time  which frequently  breaks  older  models.  To  solve this,  we  utilize  a  granular  node  by  node adapter  framework  that  handles  version transitions  seamlessly.  As  you  can  see in  the  diagram,  a  node  from  version  6 enters  the  adapter.  In  this  specific case,  the  axis  attribute  to  input adapter.  The  framework  uses  specialized patterns  to  transform  the  node,  such  as converting  attributes  directly  into inputs  to  satisfy  the  requirements  of version  7.  These  adapter  patterns  are highly  versatile.  They  can  inject  type casting  through  extend  supported  types or  even  restructure  entire  graphs.  This approach  ensures  robust  backward  and forward  compatibility,  maintaining  the integrity  of  machine  learning  models  as their  underlying  operator  sets  evolve. This  slide  details  how  hardware execution  is  managed  through  the  backend API.  As  illustrated  in  the  diagram, there  is  a  clear  separation  between  the ONNX  model  or  graph  at  the  top  and  the actual  abstract  device  execution  at  the bottom  which  includes  hardware  like  CPUs and  GPUs.  The  backend  API  acts  as  a bridge  providing  essential  decoupling. This  means  the  API  abstracts  hardware specific  details  away  from  the  model definition  allowing  for  a  portable  and flexible  architecture.  A  critical  step in  this  process  is  preparation.  The prepare  method  shown  in  the  middle  layer takes  the  generic  ONX  graph  and  compiles it  into  a  backend  rep  object.  This compilation  step  is  significant  because it  enables  one-time  hardware  specific optimizations.  By  performing  these optimizations  during  the  preparation phase,  we  ensure  that  repeated  runtime inferences  are  executed  with  maximum efficiency  on  the  target  device. We  follow  a  structured four-  stage  cycle.  We  begin  with  test definition  which  involves  setting  up node  and  model  protocols.  This  is followed  by  the  runner  phase  where models  are  downloaded  and  numpy  data  is generated.  Next,  we  proceed  to  execution to  perform  hardware  inference.  And finally,  we  move  to  reporting  using  the ONX  scoreboard.  This  approach  ensures comprehensive  coverage  ranging  from individual  node  behavior  to  massive remote  realworld  models.  A  critical component  is  our  scoreboard  integration. By  utilizing  piest  hooks,  we  can  track node  level  coverage  and  generate standardized  CSVs,  specifically nodes.csv  and  models.csv. This  allows  us  to  rank  back-end compliance  objectively  based  on empirical  data. On the  left,  the  hardware  focus  utilizes C++  to  achieve  high  performance execution,  prioritizing  raw  speed  and seamless  hardware  deployment.  On  the right,  the  truth  focus  leverages  Python and  NumPy  to  establish  a  reference evaluator  which  serves  as  the  definitive standard  for  exact  mathematical  truth. These  pure  Python  implementations provide  an  executable  specification  that acts  as  the  ultimate  ground  truth  for debugging  and  evaluating  network behavior,  ensuring  it  remains independent  of  any  hardware  specific quirks.  This  methodology  covers  a  broad range  of  domains  including  standard models,  machine  learning  components  like the  linear  classifier  and  preview training  optimizers  such  as  atom  and adagrab.  Our  approach  to  infrastructure automation  and  distribution  ensures  that our  development  life  cycle  is  both efficient  and  robust. This  integration guarantees  that  our  documentation  is always  current  and  reflects  the  latest state  of  the  codebase.  For  package distribution,  we  focus  on  the  production of  many  Linux  wheels. We  use  custom  bash  and  powershell scripts  to  build  these  critical dependencies  from  source.  This  method ensures  strict  memory  alignment  and total  compliance  with  the  C++  17 standard  maintaining  the  high performance  and  architectural  integrity required  for  our  core  infrastructure.
