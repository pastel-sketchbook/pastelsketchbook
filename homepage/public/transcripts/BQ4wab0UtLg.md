Welcome  everyone.  It  is  a  pleasure  to have  you  here  today  for  this presentation  titled  from  Luain  to  Leiden the  architecture  of  well-connected communities.  Today  we  will  explore  a structural  and  algorithmic  analysis  of community  detection  tracing  the evolution  of  these  methodologies  as  they have  become  increasingly  sophisticated. Our  journey  will  cover  the  foundational concept  of  modularity,  the  innovative application  of  the  hedonic  enim framework  to  network  science  and  the vital  importance  of  parallel  scaling specifically  through  GSP  Leiden.  By examining  these  architectural frameworks,  we  aim  to  uncover  how  we  can more  accurately  identify  well-connected communities  within  complex  networks. Let's  begin  our  deep  dive  into  these essential  algorithms.  Our  objective  is the  effective  mapping  of  network topologies,  a  process  illustrated  by transitioning  from  the  raw  network  on the  left  to  the  partition  structure  on the  right.  The  core  problem  we  address is  identifying  community  structures where  groups  of  vertices  are characterized  by  dense  internal connections  and  sparse  external  links. To  evaluate  our  success,  we  use  the modularity  Q  metric.  This  value  measures the  difference  between  the  actual fraction  of  internal  edges  within communities  compared  to  the  expected fraction  if  connections  were  randomly distributed.  While  this  metric  provides a  clear  target,  the  challenge  lies  in the  fact  that  optimizing  modularity  is  a computationally  NP  hard  problem. Therefore,  we  must  rely  on  heristic based  approaches  to  navigate  this complexity  and  uncover  the  latent organization  within  large-scale networks.  The  Louvane  method  introduced in  2008  has  become  a  cornerstone  of community  detection.  It  is  a  greedy two-phase  algorithm  focused  on  local moving  and  aggradation  to  optimize  a network's  modularity.  While  its  speed has  led  to  widespread  adoption,  it harbors  a  significant,  often  overlooked flaw.  As  illustrated  in  the  three-step process  on  the  right,  the  issue  arises during  node  migration.  In  the  initial state,  we  see  a  bridge  node  providing the  primary  connection  between  two components  of  a  community.  During  the second  step,  the  algorithm  identifies that  moving  this  bridge  node  to  an adjacent  community  will  increase  the modularity  score.  However,  this  leads  to the  fracture  shown  in  step  three.  By moving  the  node  that  provided  structural connectivity,  the  original  community  is left  internally  disconnected.  This represents  a  fundamental  algorithmic flaw.  The  result  is  that  Louvane frequently  yields  communities  that  are split  into  multiple  separate  pieces. This  structural  contradiction  is critical  as  most  downstream  analysis assumes  that  identified  communities  are cohesive  units.  When  they  are disconnected,  it  can  render  the  entire analytical  pipeline  invalid.  The  Leiden algorithm  introduced  in  2019  by  Trogue Waltman  and  Van  Egg  was  developed  to address  a  key  limitation  of  the  Louvane method,  the  presence  of  internally disconnected  clusters.  By  guaranteeing connectivity,  Leiden  provides  more structurally  robust  community  detection through  a  three-step  process  that fundamentally  alters  the  partition hierarchy.  The  first  step  is  local moving.  This  phase  uses  a  greedy optimization  approach  similar  to  the Louvane  method  where  individual  nodes are  moved  between  communities  to maximize  immediate  modularity  gain.  The second  and  most  crucial  phase  is refinement.  This  is  the  critical innovation  of  the  lighten  solution. During  refinement,  the  algorithm examines  the  communities  formed  in  the first  step  and  identifies  well-connected subcommunities.  This  process  ensures that  any  disconnected  components  within a  cluster  are  resolved,  preventing  the fragmentation  issues  often  found  in other  methods.  Finally,  the  process concludes  with  aggregation.  Here  the network  is  compressed  based  on  the refined  boundaries  established  in  the previous  step.  Nodes  within  the  same refined  community  are  collapsed  into super  nodes  and  the  entire  cycle  repeats until  no  further  improvements  can  be made.  This  ensures  a  more  accurate  and stable  partition  of  the  network.  The Leiden  algorithm  represents  a significant  advancement  in  community detection,  operating  through  a structured  three-phase  cycle  to  ensure highquality  well-connected  partitions. In  phase  one,  known  as  local  moving,  we begin  with  individual  vertices  that greedily  join  neighboring  communities. The  primary  objective  in  this  stage  is to  maximize  modularity,  forming  initial clusters  based  on  local  network  density. Moving  to  phase  two,  the  refinement stage.  The  algorithm  addresses  potential weaknesses  found  in  earlier  methods.  The partitions  established  in  the  first phase  are  dissolved  back  into singletons.  Vertices  then  make randomized,  strictly  bounded  moves within  their  original  community boundaries.  This  critical  step  allows the  algorithm  to  discover  well-connected subcommunities  and  prevents  the formation  of  poorly  connected  or disconnected  clusters.  Finally,  in  phase three,  aggregation,  the  vertices  within these  refined  partitions  are  collapsed into  supervertices.  A  key  detail  here  is that  the  initial  assignments  for  the subsequent  pass  are  derived  from  phase 1,  ensuring  the  organizational  hierarchy remains  intact.  This  entire  process iterates  as  indicated  by  the  feedback loop  until  the  network  reaches  a  stable and  optimized  community  structure.  The mechanism  of  refinement  introduces  a sophisticated  layer  of  granularity  to our  community  detection  process.  At  its core  is  the  concept  of  bounded exploration  which  ensures  that  vertices can  only  move  to  subcommunities  within their  established  phase  1  boundaries. This  constraint  prevents  nodes  from crossing  into  entirely  different  global structures,  preserving  the  macrolevel integrity  of  the  initial  clusters. Unlike  the  deterministic  greedy  approach of  phase  1,  the  refinement  stage utilizes  stochastic  movement.  By incorporating  randomness  into  these local  moves,  we  prevent  the  algorithm from  becoming  trapped  in  suboptimal configurations.  The  movement  of  a  node is  guided  by  a  specific  probability equation  where  the  likelihood  of  joining a  neighboring  sub  community  is  directly proportional  to  the  delta  modularity  of that  potential  move.  The  intensity  of this  randomness  is  governed  by  theta parameter.  By  maintaining  theta  at  a value  greater  than  zero,  the  algorithm gains  the  flexibility  needed  to  escape local  optima  and  naturally  sever disconnected  fragments  that  might  have been  overlooked  in  the  initial  phase. This  mechanism  ensures  a  more  nuanced and  accurate  representation  of  the underlying  network  architecture.  Let's examine  the  theoretical  underpinnings that  distinguish  traditional  modularity from  the  constant  pots  model  or  CPM.  On the  left  we  have  traditional  modularity often  referred  to  as  the  RB  model.  Its mathematical  framework  represented  by the  value  Q  functions  by  comparing  the internal  edge  density  of  a  partition against  a  random  expectation  network. While  widely  used,  this  model  has  a significant  limitation  known  as  the resolution  limit.  In  very  large networks,  this  approach  often  causes small  distinct  communities  to  merge artificially  because  the  null  model depends  on  the  total  number  of  edges  in the  entire  system.  On  the  right,  we  see the  constant  pots  model  which  offers  a robust  alternative.  Rather  than  relying on  a  relative  comparison  to  the  whole network,  CPM  unifies  ferromagnetic interactions  which  act  as  rewards  for links  and  anti-feromagnetic  interactions which  serve  as  penalties  for  non-links. The  primary  advantage  here  is  that  CPM utilizes  an  absolute  resolution parameter  that  is  independent  of  network size,  effectively  solving  the  resolution limit  issue.  The  granularity  of  the partition  is  controlled  by  the  gamma parameter.  Adjusting  this  value  allows for  precise  control.  A  higher  gamma results  in  more  numerous  smaller communities  while  a  lower  gamma  yields fewer  larger  communities.  This  diagram illustrates  the  fundamental  trade-off that  occurs  whenever  a  node  considers changing  its  position  within  a  network. In  what  we  define  as  a  hyonic  game,  each node  acts  as  an  autonomous  agent, weighing  the  potential  gain  of additional  friends  against  the inevitable  penalty  of  strangers.  On  the left  side  of  our  balance,  we  have  the friends  variable  represented  as  delta  D. This  constitutes  the  positive connections  within  a  partition.  On  the right  we  have  strangers  denoted  as  delta d  hat  representing  the  negative  friction or  lack  of  familiarity.  To  quantify  this relationship  we  utilize  the  familiarity index  or  f  subi  as  shown  in  the  formula. This  index  is  calculated  as  the  ratio  of friends  to  the  total  sum  of  friends  and strangers.  This  index  serves  as  the critical  threshold  for  the  resolution decision.  The  decision  logic  is determined  by  the  variable  gamma.  If gamma  is  less  than  the  familiarity index,  the  node  prioritizes  gaining friends.  Conversely,  if  gamma  is  greater than  the  index,  the  node  prioritizes avoiding  strangers.  Ultimately,  we  are looking  for  fully  robust  partitions. This  describes  a  state  of  equilibrium where  every  node  in  the  system simultaneously  maximizes  its  number  of friends  while  minimizing  its  number  of strangers,  resulting  in  a  stable  and optimized  network  structure.  When evaluating  community  detection algorithms,  we  can  see  a  clear  evolution in  mathematical  guarantees  from  Luane  to Leiden.  Both  algorithms  successfully provide  gamma  separation  and  node optimality,  ensuring  that  communities are  not  unnecessarily  merged  and  that individual  nodes  are  assigned  to  their most  optimal  positions.  However,  the lighten  algorithm  introduces  critical improvements  in  areas  where  Luvane  falls short.  Specifically,  Lighten  guarantees gamma  connectivity,  ensuring  internal paths  exist  between  all  nodes  and uniform  gamma  density,  which  prevents subsets  from  being  separated  from  the larger  community.  Most  significantly, Leiden  achieves  subset  optimality, ensuring  all  subsets  are  locally optimally  assigned.  This  is  the strongest  mathematical  guarantee available,  ensuring  that  partitions  are highly  stable  and  reliable,  which fundamentally  positions  Leiden  as  a strict  upgrade  over  the  Louvane algorithm.  We  are  examining  the persisting  anomaly,  Leiden's  edge  case. A  primary  concern  is  the  blind  spot. Although  the  refinement  phase  resolves initial  disconnections,  it  cannot  fully prevent  new  disconnected  subcommunities from  emerging  within  set  boundaries  as iterations  progress.  This  vulnerability feeds  into  the  aggregation  trap  where these  new  disconnections  may  persist into  the  supervertex  graph  depending  on the  aggregation  methods  between  phase  1 and  phase  2. GSP  lighten addresses  the  critical  challenge  of parallel  scaling  and  community detection.  Standard  lighten implementations  like  lib  lighten  alg  are inherently  sequential  which  leads  to significant  computational  bottlenecks when  dealing  with  massive  graphs containing  billions  of  edges.  To  solve this,  we  introduced  GSP  Leiden,  which stands  for  split  pass  lighten.  This  is  a highly  parallelized  multi-core implementation  utilizing  OpenMP.  It  is designed  to  operate  at  massive  scale while  providing  a  crucial  guarantee,  the elimination  of  disconnected  communities. The  core  of  this  innovation  is  the  SPFS mechanism.  This  approach  is  proactive. Rather  than  waiting  for  a post-processing  pass  at  the  end,  GSP Lighten  intervenes  during  every  single pass  of  the  algorithm,  it  utilizes  a breath  first  search  technique  for partitioning.  During  this  phase,  threads select  random  vertices  and  label  all reachable  vertices.
