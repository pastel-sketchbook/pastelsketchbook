Welcome  everyone.  Today  we're  diving into  From  Blueprint  to  battlefield,  a definitive  guide  to  secure  federated identity.  In  this  session,  we  will explore  the  critical  journey  of  moving identity  systems  from  the  design  phase to  real  world  deployment.  We'll  examine how  to  architect  and  stress  test  these systems  by  leveraging  NIST  standards  and rigorous  formal  analysis.  Our  goal  is  to ensure  that  federated  identity frameworks  are  not  only  theoretically sound  but  resilient  enough  to  withstand the  complexities  of  today's  security landscape.  Let's  get  started.  Federated identity  has  become  the  essential backbone  of  the  modern  web.  It  allows relying  parties,  the  apps  and  services we  use  everyday  to  delegate  user authentication  to  trusted  identity providers  like  Google,  Microsoft  or PayPal.  This  is  the  foundation  of  the seamless  single  sign  on  experience  that users  have  come  to  expect.  The  industry standard  powering  this  is  open  ID connect  or  OIDC.  Built  on  top  of  OOTH 2.0,  OIDC  provides  a  framework  for securing  authentication  through cryptographically  signed  tokens. However,  while  it  is  highly  efficient, it  is  also  incredibly  complex.  As illustrated  by  the  tangled  interactions in  this  diagram,  the  multi-party  nature of  federation  creates  a  large  and  often misunderstood  attack  surface,  OIDC's inherent  flexibility  combined  with  its many  optional  features  and  complex browser  interactions  has  been  shown  to be  vulnerable  to  subtle  but  devastating attacks,  risks  that  are  frequently overlooked  by  informal  security evaluations.  Moving  on  to  the foundational  architecture,  NIST SP800-63C provides  the  authoritative  model  for identity  federation.  This  framework  is built  around  three  core  actors.  The subscriber  who  is  the  end  user  seeking access.  The  identity  provider  or  IDP which  manages  the  user's  identity  and performs  authentication  and  the  relying party  or  RP  which  consumes  that  identity information  to  deliver  a  service.  As shown  in  the  diagram,  the  workflow typically  involves  redirects  between  the subscriber  and  the  IDP  for  primary authentication.  Once  the  user  is verified,  the  IDP  passes  the  necessary identity  information  to  the  RP,  which then  establishes  and  manages  the  user session.  This  separation  of  duties  is the  cornerstone  of  the  model.  The  IDP handles  the  heavy  lifting  of authentication  while  the  RP  securely relies  on  that  result  to  grant  access. In  this  framework,  the  assertion  serves as  the  keystone,  the  central  component that  holds  the  entire  identity architecture  together.  It  acts  as  the bridge  between  the  identity  provider  and the  relying  party,  securely  packaging the  claims  needed  to  link  authentication with  authorization.  As  you  can  see  in our  metadata  list,  this  isn't  just  a simple  data  transfer.  It  is  a  highly secured  package.  Key  elements  like  the audience  prevent  redirect  attacks  by ensuring  the  assertion  is  only  used where  intended  while  the  signature provides  cryptographic  proof  of authenticity.  We  also  include  strict time  bounds  and  unique  identifiers  to mitigate  replay  attacks  and  ensure  the information  remains  fresh.  Ultimately, as  NIST  SP800-63C notes,  the  assertion  is  what  allows  the relying  party  to  verify  exactly  who  a subscriber  is  and  make  the  critical authorization  decisions  required  to grant  access  to  resources.  Without  this keystone,  the  trust  relationship  between the  IDP  and  RP  simply  cannot  function. Now,  let's  look  at  federation  assurance levels  or  fowls.  These  categories measure  how  much  trust  we  can  place  in the  security  of  an  identity  assertion. Starting  with  FAL1,  our  baseline  good level.  This  requires  a  bearer  assertion signed  by  the  identity  provider.  While the  signature  protects  the  data  from being  tampered  with,  the  assertion itself  isn't  encrypted.  This  means  that if  an  attacker  intercepts  it,  simple possession  is  enough  to  impersonate  the user.  FAL2  represents  the  better standard.  Here  the  assertion  is  both signed  by  the  IDP  and  encrypted specifically  for  the  relying  party.  This protects  the  data  from  both  modification and  disclosure  while  in  transit.  Because of  this  added  layer  of  privacy,  FAL2  is mandatory  for  any  front  channel  flows where  the  assertion  passes  through  a browser.  Finally,  we  have  FAL3  which  is the  best  and  most  secure  option.  This moves  us  to  a  holder  of  key  model.  Not only  is  the  assertion  signed  and encrypted,  but  the  subscriber  must  also cryptographically  prove  they  possess  a specific  key  bound  to  that  assertion. This  effectively  prevents  impersonation even  in  the  event  that  the  assertion itself  is  stolen,  providing  the  highest level  of  protection  against  identity theft.  Now,  let's  look  at  Open  ID Connect  or  OIDC,  which  has  become  the industry  standard  for  federated authentication.  It's  important  to clarify  that  OIDC  is  not  simply  an evolution  of  the  original  Open  ID. Rather,  it  is  a  dedicated  identity  layer built  directly  on  top  of  the  OOTH  2.0 authorization  framework.  Before  OIDC, developers  often  used  OOTH  for authentication  in  an  ad  hoc  and sometimes  insecure  manner.  OIDC formalizes  this  process  by  introducing standardized  flows  and  crucially  the  ID token.  The  ID  token  serves  as  a  NIST compliant  assertion.  It  is  a cryptographically  signed  JSON  web  token or  JWT  that  contains  specific  claims about  the  authentication  event  itself. Beyond  just  fixing  authentication  for OOTH,  OIDC  adds  critical  features necessary  for  scale.  This  includes discovery  for  automatically  finding identity  provider  endpoints  and  dynamic client  registration  for  on  the-fly  setup of  relying  parties.  As  FET  custers  and Schmidz  noted  in  2017,  OIDC  wasn't  just about  retrofitting  authentication  into OOTH  with  secure  tokens.  It  was  designed to  enable  these  robust  additional features  that  make  modern  federated identity  possible.  Now,  let's  walk through  the  anatomy  of  a  secure  login, specifically  the  OIDC  authorization  code flow.  This  process  involves  three  main entities.  the  user's  browser,  the relying  party  or  RP,  which  is  the application  you  are  trying  to  access, and  the  identity  provider  known  as  the IDP.  The  process  begins  in  the  front channel,  which  is  mediated  through  the browser.  First,  the  user  initiates  a login  request  at  the  application.  The  RP then  redirects  the  browser  to  the identity  provider,  passing  along  a client  ID  and  a  unique  state  parameter. Once  the  user  successfully  authenticates at  the  IDP,  the  provider  redirects  the browser  back  to  the  application  with  a one-time  authorization  code.  Note  that at  this  stage,  the  actual  identity tokens  haven't  been  shared  yet.  This leads  us  to  the  back  channel,  which  is  a direct  secure  server  to  server connection.  The  RP  sends  that authorization  code  along  with  its private  client  secret  directly  to  the IDP's  token  endpoint.  After  the  IDP validates  these  credentials,  it  finally returns  the  ID  token  and  access  token  to the  application.  There  are  two  critical security  components  to  highlight  here. First,  the  use  of  the  back  channel ensures  that  sensitive  identity  tokens are  never  exposed  to  the  browser, mitigating  the  risk  of  interception. Second,  the  state  parameter  acts  as  a CSRF  token,  linking  the  initial  request to  the  final  response  to  prevent  session injection  attacks.  This  separation  of duties  makes  the  authorization  code  flow the  gold  standard  for  secure  web authentication.  Moving  from  theoretical blueprints  to  the  digital  battlefield, we  find  that  even  standards  compliant protocols  like  OIDC  can  harbor  hidden risks.  We  conducted  a  rigorous  doll  of Yao  style  formal  analysis  of  these multi-party  interactions  and  the  results were  revealing.  As  you  can  see  in  this flow  diagram,  there  are  multiple  points where  the  front  and  back  channels  can  be compromised.  Our  adversary  model  is robust,  assuming  an  attacker  who  can intercept  network  traffic,  host malicious  sites,  and  deceive  users  into initiating  flawed  login  flows.  However, the  most  critical  takeaway  here  is  that the  most  severe  vulnerabilities  aren't cryptographic  breaks.  The  encryption itself  is  solid.  Instead,  the  real threats  are  logical  flaws  that  confuse one  of  the  parties  about  the  state  or context  of  the  authentication.  It's  not a  failure  of  math,  but  a  failure  of coordination  between  the  browser,  the relying  party,  and  the  identity provider.  Now,  let's  examine  a  critical vulnerability  known  as  the  IDP  mixup attack.  This  attack  is  particularly dangerous  because  it  tricks  a  REIM  party or  RP  into  leaking  valid  user credentials  by  confusing  it  about  which identity  provider  it  is  communicating with.  On  the  left,  we  see  what  the  RP thinks  is  happening.  A  standard  login attempt  where  it  receives  an authorization  code  from  an  attacker controlled  IDP  and  sends  it  back  to their  endpoint  for  verification.  But look  at  the  reality  on  the  right.  The attacker  first  intercepts  the  initial request  and  redirects  the  user  to  a legitimate  honest  IDP.  The  user authenticates  normally  and  the  honest IDP  issues  a  perfectly  valid authorization  code.  However,  because  the RP  has  been  confused  about  the  session's origin,  it  sends  that  valid  high-value code  directly  to  the  attacker's  token endpoint.  The  end  result  is  a  critical credential  leak.  By  the  time  the  RP realizes  something  is  wrong,  the attacker  already  has  the  authorization code  needed  to  hijack  the  user  session. Next,  let's  examine  how  serverside request  forgery  or  SSRF  can  turn federation  endpoints  into  attack proxies.  We've  identified  two  primary threat  vectors  here.  The  first  involves attacking  the  relying  party.  During  the OIDC  discovery  process,  an  RP  fetches configuration  data  from  URLs  provided  by a  discovery  service.  If  an  attacker  sets up  a  malicious  service  that  responds with  internal  network  addresses,  they can  trick  the  RP  server  into  scanning its  own  internal  network  or  triggering unauthorized  actions.  The  second  is  a novel  attack  vector  targeting  the identity  provider.  OIDC  allows authorization  parameters  to  be  passed  by reference  via  a  request  URI  by  crafting a  request  that  points  this  URI  toward  an internal  service.  An  attacker  forces  the IDP  to  make  requests  on  their  behalf, effectively  bypassing  traditional perimeter  security.  The  key  takeaway  is that  both  relying  parties  and  identity providers  must  treat  any  URL  received from  an  external  party  as  untrusted input.  To  mitigate  these  risks,  it  is critical  to  implement  strict  URL filtering  and  validation.  Moving  on, let's  examine  a  catalog  of  common vulnerabilities  that  frequently  impact federation  flows.  First,  we  have  state parameter  leakage  or  misuse.  This  occurs when  the  state  parameter  is  either omitted  or  reused  across  multiple  login attempts.  This  is  critical  because  it leaves  the  door  open  for  cross-sight request  forgery,  CSRF,  or  session swapping  attacks,  potentially  allowing an  attacker  to  log  a  user  into  an account  they  control.  Next  is  code  or token  leakage  via  the  referral  header. If  a  redirect  endpoint  includes  external resources  like  images  or  scripts,  the browser's  referer  header  can inadvertently  leak  the  full  URL, including  sensitive  codes  or  tokens  to third  party  sites,  leading  to  credential theft.  We  also  see  parameter  injection and  pollution.  In  this  scenario,  an attacker  injects  duplicate  parameters such  as  multiple  redirect  URI  values.  If the  server  validates  one  but  uses another,  the  attacker  can  hijack  the flow  and  steal  the  user's  tokens. Finally,  there's  the  HTTP  307  redirect attack.  Because  a  307  redirect  preserves the  original  HTTP  method,  it  can  cause the  browser  to  repost  a  user's  raw credentials  directly  to  a  malicious relying  party's  URI  after authentication.  Understanding  these pitfalls  is  essential  for  building  and maintaining  secure  federated  identity systems.  Let's  dive  into  what  we  call the  building  code.  Our  formal  analysis has  proven  that  a  specific  set  of defensive  measures  is  both  effective  and sufficient  to  mitigate  identity  threats. We've  distilled  these  findings  into  the three  pillars  of  secure  federation. First,  we  must  secure  the  assertion. This  means  protecting  the  core  data package  to  ensure  it  cannot  be  tampered with  or  inspected  by  unauthorized parties.  Second,  we  validate  every  step of  the  flow.  We  treat  every  incoming message  and  parameter  as  potentially hostile,  verifying  its  integrity  and context  at  every  stage  of  the  process. Finally,  we  isolate  and  sanitize.  This involves  enforcing  a  strict  separation of  domains  and  treating  all  cross  party data  as  untrusted  by  default. Implementing  this  building  code  is essential  for  any  secure  federated system,  ensuring  that  your infrastructure  is  truly  code  compliant and  resilient  against  attack.  Our  first guideline  focuses  on  securing  the assertion  through  robust  cryptography and  scoping.  To  do  this  effectively,  you must  apply  the  federation  assurance level  or  FAL  that  is  appropriate  for your  specific  context.  First,  under FAL1,  we  must  always  sign.  Every assertion  must  be  signed  by  the  identity provider  using  either  asymmetric  keys  or a  per  RP  symmetric  key.  This  ensures  the relying  party  can  validate  the  signature which  is  our  primary  defense  against  the manufacturer  or  modification  of assertions.  Next,  for  FAL2,  we  encrypt in  the  front  channel.  If  an  assertion  is passed  through  a  browser  such  as  an  OIDC implicit  or  hybrid  flows,  it  must  be encrypted  for  the  specific  RP.  This prevents  sensitive  assertion  data  from being  disclosed  to  the  browser  or intercepted  by  network  attackers.  Beyond cryptography,  we  must  enforce  strict audience  restriction.  The  assertions audience  field  must  clearly  identify  the specific  relying  party  and  that  RP  must verify  it  is  indeed  the  intended audience.  This  simple  check  is  critical for  preventing  assertion  redirect attacks.  Finally,  we  recommend  short lifetimes.  By  setting  expiration  times to  just  a  few  seconds  or  minutes,  we significantly  narrow  the  window  of opportunity  for  an  attacker  to  attempt an  assertion  reuse.  By  following  these four  principles,  you  create  a  hardened environment  that  protects  both  the integrity  and  the  confidentiality  of your  federated  identity  system.  Moving on  to  guideline  two,  validate  the  state and  context  of  every  protocol  step.  This is  critical  because  some  of  the  most dangerous  attacks  exploit  a  mismatch between  the  expected  state  and  the actual  state  of  a  request.  To  secure this  flow,  there  are  several  mandatory validations  we  must  implement.  First,  to mitigate  identity  mixup  attacks,  the identity  provider  must  include  its issuer  identifier  in  the  response  and the  relying  party  must  verify  that  this matches  the  IDP  it  originally  initiated the  flow  with.  Next,  we  must  bind sessions  using  a  unique  unguessable state  parameter.  This  value  needs  to  be tied  to  the  user's  specific  login session  and  used  only  once.  Similarly, authorization  codes  must  be  invalidated immediately  after  their  first  use  at  the token  endpoint  to  prevent  replay attacks.  From  a  browser  security perspective,  prevent  referer  leakage  by setting  a  no  referer  policy  on  redirect pages  and  avoiding  third  party  content. It  is  also  vital  to  sanitize  all  inputs, treating  parameters  like  redirect  URIs as  hostile  and  validating  them  against  a strict  whitelist  to  prevent  injection and  SSIF  attacks.  Finally,  ensure  you are  using  safe  redirects.  Use  HTTP  303  C other  for  post  authentication  redirects rather  than  307  to  prevent  the accidental  rescending  of  form  data.  When we  talk  about  secure  federation,  we  are really  talking  about  the  disciplined application  of  least  surprise.  The  core principle  to  understand  is  that  the  most sophisticated  attacks  today  rarely target  cryptography.  Instead,  they exploit  the  delta,  the  gap  between  a component's  assumed  state  and  its  actual state.  We  see  this  in  three  common scenarios.  First,  the  IDP  mixup  where  a relying  party  assumes  it  is communicating  with  IDPA  but  the  user  is actually  interacting  with  IDPB. Second,  SSRF  where  an  IDP  assumes  a request  URI  is  safe  when  it  actually points  to  a  sensitive  internal  service. And  third,  session  management  failures where  a  user  assumes  they've  logged  out but  their  session  remains  active  at  the IDP.  As  architects,  your  goal  is  to eliminate  these  deltas.  You  must rigorously  validate  every  assumption, enforce  explicit  state  transitions,  and ensure  that  every  component  in  the  chain has  a  provably  correct  view  of  the transaction.  Always  remember,  the blueprint  is  your  guide,  but  the  code  is the  law.  Build  accordingly.
