## Summary

A blueprint for orbital data centers, examining the thermodynamics, architecture, and economics of moving compute infrastructure off Earth. The video presents a reference design with 18 modular compute units, 400 kW solar arrays, specialized 1,200-watt thermal radiators, and station-keeping thrusters. It then contrasts terrestrial constraints (24% solar capacity factor, water-intensive cooling, land/carbon footprint) against orbital advantages (~100% solar capacity, vacuum isolation, cryogenic deep-space heat sink) and details a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit configuration as the optimal deployment for continuous solar exposure.

## Key Takeaways

- Reference orbital data center: 18 modular compute units + high-density storage, 400 kW solar generation, 1,200 W thermal radiator dissipation, high-gain data antenna, and orbital maneuvering thrusters.
- Terrestrial solar runs at ~24% capacity factor in the US, requiring massive battery storage; orbit delivers near 100% capacity, eliminating storage burden.
- Cooling shifts from water-intensive convection loops on Earth to radiation-only heat rejection in vacuum — a fundamental thermal management redesign.
- Connectivity trade-off: terrestrial fiber gives ultra-low latency, while orbital systems rely on optical inter-satellite links and accept physical data shuttle delays.
- Maintenance evolves from rapid human intervention to autonomous fault management with significant fleet redundancy; physical isolation provides natural air-gapped security.
- A dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit keeps satellites in continuous sunlight along the terminator, maximizing solar exposure for the compute payload.
