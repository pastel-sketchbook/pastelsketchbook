# Decoding the US Housing Market

## Summary

This session decodes the US housing market into 2026-2027 through three structural shifts — national stagnation, regional reversal, and wage-rent decoupling. It crunches the institutional rate-and-price forecasts (7.4-7.5% mortgage floor compressing appreciation to 0.6% by 2027), maps the four-region divergence from surging Midwest affordability to Sunbelt oversupply corrections, dissects the wage-rent equation (255% income growth vs 415% price growth since 1985, price-to-income 3.5→5.0, rents +30% vs wages +20% post-2019), contrasts elastic Austin with inelastic New York, compares US elasticity and labor mobility against Korea's Seoul concentration and jeonse leverage, and closes on hyper-local underwriting strategy.

## Key Takeaways

- Expect stagnation, not collapse: Fannie Mae pins the 30-year rate between 7.4-7.5% through late 2027, decelerating appreciation from 8% to 2.7% in 2026 and just 0.6% in 2027 (Zillow ~1.2%, with downside at -0.2%) — no major institution forecasts a systemic crash, only an extended, rate-capped cooling with locked-in borrowing costs suppressing transactions.
- Trade the map, not the average: Midwest leads at +1-4% on the tightest inventory (Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Indianapolis plus breakouts like Rockford and Syracuse); Northeast holds 0-2% on supply floors (New York, Boston, Philadelphia); Sunbelt corrects -2 to -6% on completed-inventory waves hitting eroded purchasing power (Austin -6.1%, New Orleans -4.8%, Florida metros near critical inventory); West stagnates at -1 to +1% under high-price rate lock-in (LA, SF, Seattle, Phoenix) — the national average is obsolete.
- Read the wage-rent ledger: median incomes +255% since 1985 while prices rose +415%, pushing the price-to-income ratio from 3.5 to 5.0; post-2019 rents surged ~30% against ~20% wage growth in 44 of the 50 largest metros, and only the 2024-2026 apartment-supply wave let wages begin closing the gap in select markets.
- Understand the wage-rent transmission: powerful economic engines pull premium compensation, migration inflows collide with zoning- or geography-capped housing supply, and rents rebalance until they absorb the wage premium — high-wage cities converge to high-rent cities unless supply responds.
- Apply the supply-elasticity rule: elastic markets (Austin) flood demand with new construction so the worker keeps the wage gains; inelastic markets (New York, Boston) trigger bidding wars that transfer productivity gains to property owners — when supply is flexible, the worker wins; when it's choked, the landlord wins.
- Contrast US vs Korea structure: the US spreads demand across elastic regional hubs with mobile labor chasing wage-to-rent ratios and yield-based monthly leases, cooling gradually through rates and inventory; Korea concentrates capital in Seoul with anchored populations and a jeonse deposit system that weds housing to private leverage — policy-driven swings and double-digit spikes vs the US's slow grind.
- Underwrite locally: no national rebound is coming before 2027, regional decoupling rewards capital deployed into inventory-starved Midwest/Northeast fundamentals over oversupplied Sunbelt corridors, and affordability improves most where inventory arrives fastest — success belongs to metro- and neighborhood-level operators.
