## Summary

The Anatomy of a Giant is a botanical dossier on massive watermelons, tracing their global cultivation (Iranian Corusan, Chinese giant, Texas Black Diamond), the genetics behind color, sweetness, and seedlessness, and the biological blueprint enabling their scale. It connects modern yield strategy to an evolutionary survival mechanism inherited from drought-adapted African land races.

## Key Takeaways

- Extreme heat, extended growing seasons, and specialized cultivars (Carolina Cross, Diarbakir) make regions like Texas ideal for large-scale watermelon production.
- Texas correlation data shows fruit weight — not fruit count or diameter — is the single trait significantly correlated with higher yield, favoring fewer but larger melons.
- Flesh color and sweetness are genetically linked: shifting carotenoid accumulation toward beta-carotene and xanthophylls (yellow/orange flesh) biologically correlates with higher sugar than red lycopine varieties.
- The LCYB (lycopene beta-cyclase) gene switches flesh red vs. yellow, while PSY1 (phytoene synthase) controls carotenoid accumulation rate; QTLs govern rind pattern and thickness.
- Seedless fruit is engineered by crossing diploid (2n) and tetraploid (4n) lineages to produce a sterile triploid (3n) whose chromosome imbalance blocks meiosis.
- Giant size is an evolutionary echo — large fruit evolved in wild African ancestors (C. mucosospermus, C. amarus) as a water-storage survival adaptation, now leveraged for high-heat agricultural yield.
